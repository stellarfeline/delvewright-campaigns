# The Drowned Bell — story

The fiction of record. Every later document is judged against this one, and this
one is judged against `concept/`.

## The place

**Halgrave** is a rock off a drowned coast, joined to the mainland only by a
causeway the sea uncovers twice a day. On it stands a priory that is also a
fort: a shore of graves, a cliff road, a gatehouse, an outer ward, a chapel
cloister, a hall and keep, a cistern cut into the rock, and a bell tower.

The coast behind it has no dry ground to bury anyone in. For four hundred years
the dead of that coast were ferried out to Halgrave at low water and laid in the
rock. The priory took a fee for the crossing, a fee for the ground, and a fee for
the tolling. Its wealth was other people's dying, and it was very good at it.

## The bell

The bell is named **Mercy**. It hangs in the open belfry and it is the only thing
on the rock anyone on the mainland can hear.

Around its lip: **I CALL THE LIVING · I CARRY THE DEAD · I ANSWER THE LAST HAND.**

Mercy is a clock, not an ornament. The causeway is invisible under water for
nineteen hours a day, and the coast reads the tide by ear:

| toll | meaning |
|---|---|
| **one** | a body is coming across. Clear the road. |
| **two** | the way is shut. Do not set foot on the flat. |
| **three** | the way is open. Come across. |

A pilgrim, a mourner or a corpse crossed on a **passage-token** — a lead badge
stamped with three tolls, bought at the mainland shore. The token is a promise
in metal: *present this, and the bell will ring you across.*

## The Long Tide

Fifty-one years ago, plague came up the coast and the coast came to Halgrave —
not with its dead, with itself. Several hundred living people stood on the
mainland strand at dusk with tokens in their fists, waiting for three tolls,
and the causeway was dry and open beneath them.

**Prior Ancel Wray** ordered two.

Two tolls is *the way is shut*. The crowd on the strand held. The ones already
out on the flat — half a mile of them, in the dark, between the tide-stakes —
turned around and walked back into water that was, by then, coming in behind
them. The lie was not silence. Halgrave rang, and rang the wrong number, and its
answer was believed because the bell had never lied before.

**Odo Ferrier**, who rowed the dead out for thirty years, put his boat in and
carried them off the flat four at a time until the boat went over. The wrecks are
still in the outer ward, where the water later came to rest.

When the surviving brothers understood what a correction from the belfry would
mean — that the coast would learn the toll had been a decision — they finished
it. They struck out the bell's **tongue** and dropped it down the cistern well.
They cut the rope. They broke the first flight of the belfry stair. Mercy has
not spoken since.

The sea kept the debt. The tide came into the outer ward that winter and never
fully went out. The priory emptied. The rock did not.

## The answered

The drowned of the Long Tide come up out of the flat at every low water and
climb. They are not vengeful and they are not looking for anyone. They are
going to the belfry, because three tolls were owed to them and never given, and
a thing owed is a road. They cannot ring it — no tongue, no rope, no stair — so
they reach the break, and fall, and come up with the next tide and climb again.
Fifty-one years of that has worn most of them out of any shape you could talk
to. Two are left who can still hold a conversation. One is still on the stair.

They are hostile the way a current is hostile: you are in the way and they do
not stop. Nothing on this rock hates the player.

## What the player is

You wake face-down on the Barrow Flat at the lowest water in fifty-one years,
with a lead passage-token closed in your fist and nothing behind you but water.

You are one of the answered. You were on the flat that night, you heard two
tolls, you turned around, and you have been down there since. What is different
about you tonight is only that you woke up further along than the rest: you have
your hands, your name is still on you somewhere, and the causeway is dry for the
first time since the night it killed you.

The delve never states this in an opening crawl. It is available three ways —
the priory's crossing-ledger (Z2, optional), the prior's confession (Z5,
optional), and the ferryman's tally in his own wreck (Z3, optional) — and the
last of the three has your name on its final line, uncrossed. A player who
skips all three finishes the delve without ever being told, which is correct.

Co-operative play needs no special account: everyone who came up this tide came
up on the same errand.

## Why climb

Because the bell can still be rung, and it is the only thing on this rock that
can be changed. Everyone the player meets wants it rung, or wants it never rung
again, and no two of them mean the same thing by it.

## The last hand

*I answer the last hand.* Mercy gives whatever number the last person to ring
it gives, and the coast believes it. That is the ending, and it is a choice of a
number, made at the bell:

- **Three.** *The way is open.* The debt is paid in the only currency it was ever
  owed in. The answered walk down off the rock and across the dry causeway, and
  the player goes with them, because the player is one of them. Halgrave is left
  empty and quiet for the first time in fifty-one years.
- **Two.** *The way is shut.* Ancel's toll, rung a second time by someone who
  knows exactly what it costs. The answered turn around on the stair. The rock
  keeps them, and keeps the player, who now owns it.
- **Silence.** Fell the bell — cut the frame and let Mercy go through the tower
  floor into the cistern. Nothing is ever answered again by anyone. The one
  ending in which the player is not lied to and does not lie.

The optional strands do not add a fourth ending. They change **who is standing
there** when it is rung — whether Odo is beaten or standing aside, whether Hask
got off the rock, whether Ide finished her round, whether Emeric came up the
stair at last — and they change what the bell is rung *with*, which is the whole
point of Sister Ide's rite.

## Themes, stated plainly so they can be checked

- **An institution's worst act is a memo.** Nobody on Halgrave was a monster.
  A man in a chair chose a number, a boy carried the order, a founder struck out
  a tongue, and several hundred people walked back into the sea.
- **The dead are not the horror; the bookkeeping is.** The answered are
  pitiable in every encounter and the level says so — they climb the way the
  player climbs, and they are all going where the player is going.
- **Everything on this rock is a promise in a physical object**: a lead token, a
  ledger line, a tally with an uncrossed name, a bell with a sentence on its lip.
  Each of them is kept or broken by the last hand that touches it.
